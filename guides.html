Practical guides for people experiencing homelessness and mental health challenges

Browse our practical guides for advice on staying safe, finding shelter, claiming benefits and more. Click the topics on the left to get started.

Whether you're going through something right now, planning ahead, or supporting someone you care about this is a space with no judgement. Our guides are written to give you clear, honest information about some of the hardest situations people face.

Not sure where to start? You might find it helpful to:

What to do if you have nowhere safe to stay tonight

If tonight is your first night outside, this guide will help you stay safe, find warmth and know what to do next. Remember, you matter and tonight you deserve somewhere safe.

A few important things to know

  • You cannot be turned away without an assessment. If a council tries to turn you away without assessing your situation, you have the right to ask them to reconsider and to make a formal complaint.
  • Rough sleeping in cold weather is a serious health risk. Please don't wait and reach out tonight.
  • You are not a burden. These services exist for exactly this reason.

Call or contact council ASAP

Every local council in England has a legal duty to help people who are homeless or at risk of becoming homeless. You don't need to have already slept rough to ask for help. If you have nowhere to go tonight, that counts.

When you call, tell them:

  • You have nowhere safe to sleep tonight
  • Whether you have any children with you
  • Any health conditions or disabilities, this affects how they must respond to you

If you're already on the street

Porchlight runs outreach in Kent and can help connect you to emergency accommodation. Call 0800 567 7699 (Lines are open Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm).

Shelter England also has an emergency helpline 0808 800 4444 (8am-5pm weekdays).

Both are free.

If you feel unsafe right now

If you are in immediate danger from another person, the weather or from yourself call 999 (or dial 18000).

If you need support but it's not an emergency, call 111 (or dial 18001).

How to stay safe when you're couch surfing

Staying at someone else's place when you have no home of your own (like moving between friends, family, or acquaintances) is often called "hidden homelessness". It's still homelessness and you still have rights.

Know that you are still legally homeless

Many people don't realise that couch surfing counts as homelessness in the eyes of the law. You can approach your local council for housing support even if you technically have a roof over your head tonight. You don't need to have slept rough.

Tell the council you are "sofa surfing with no fixed abode" they are obliged to assess your situation.

Protect your physical safety

  • Trust your instincts. If you feel unsafe in a home, you don't have to stay. No favour is worth your safety.
  • Tell someone where you are. Even a text to a friend or family member saying "I'm staying at [name]'s place" can be a lifeline.
  • Don't leave your belongings somewhere you can't get back to easily. Try to keep essential documents (ID, bank cards, any medication) with you at all times.
  • Have an exit plan. Before you settle somewhere, think: if I needed to leave quickly, could I?

Be aware of exploitation

Some people take advantage of those who are couch surfing. This can include:

  • Being pressured into sexual activity in exchange for accommodation ("survival sex")
  • Being asked to commit crimes (e.g. accepting parcels, holding money, dealing drugs) as "payment" for a place to stay
  • Emotional manipulation, coercive control, or threats

None of this is okay. If you're in this situation, it is not your fault. You can talk to Porchlight confidentially at 0800 567 7699 (Lines are open Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm. Calls are free.), or contact the police on 101 (or 999 if you're in immediate danger).

Look after your mental health

  • Try to keep some routine, even small things like a regular sleep time or eating at the same time each day
  • Be honest with yourself about how you're feeling and see our mental health guides if things feel difficult
  • Reach out to your GP even without a permanent address.

Registering with a GP without a fixed address

You have the right to register with a GP even if you have no permanent address. You can use a day centre, a friend's address, or the GP surgery's own address as your contact. Ask the surgery to make a note that you are of "no fixed abode." They cannot refuse to register you on the grounds of having no address.

Keep seeking longer-term support

  • Contact your local council's housing team
  • Ask about local hostels and supported accommodation through Porchlight: 0800 567 7699
  • Look into whether you may be eligible for benefits (see our guide on claiming benefits)

Looking after yourself while homeless

This guide is about the practical day-to-day: staying physically well, getting food, keeping clean, and protecting your mental health. None of these things should feel like luxuries they're necessities, and there is help available.

Food

Foodbanks in Kent are widely available and free. You usually need a referral from a professional (GP, social worker, housing officer), but many will help you directly if you explain you're in urgent need. Find your nearest at our Interactive Map.

Day centres and drop-ins run by Porchlight and other organisations often provide hot meals, drinks, and company. Call 0800 567 7699 or check our interactive map to find one near you.

If you're really hungry right now, most churches, mosques, and community centres run food projects, walking in and asking is completely okay.

Water, washing and toilets

  • Libraries and day centres usually have free toilets and washing facilities
  • Many leisure centres offer free or low-cost showers for people who are homeless. You can ask Porchlight or your local council about schemes in your area
  • Supermarkets and fast food restaurants have toilets available to customers

Staying warm

  • Severe Weather Emergency Protocol (SWEP) is activated when temperatures are forecast to drop to 0°C or below councils must provide emergency shelter. Call your council or Porchlight to find out what's available
  • Libraries, shopping centres, and churches can provide shelter from the cold during the day
  • Wear layers, but not just one thick coat, trapped air between layers keeps you much warmer
  • Sleeping on cardboard insulates you from cold ground

Your health

You can still see a GP. Register with a local surgery without a fixed address (see the couch surfing guide for how). Always mention to the GP that you are homeless as this can affect what support they can arrange for you.

Dental care is available on the NHS. Practices registered with the NHS can often see you as an emergency even without being a registered patient.

If you use drugs or alcohol, non-judgemental support is available. In Kent, contact Change, Grow, Live (CGL) on 0300 303 7029 or visit www.changegrowlive.org.

Mental health

  • Talk to your GP about how you're feeling
  • Contact the Samaritans any time on 116 123 (free, 24/7) just to have someone to talk to
  • If you're in crisis, call the Kent and Medway Mental Health Crisis Line: 0800 107 0160 (24 hours, free)

Keeping your belongings safe

  • Try to keep important documents (ID, letters, bank cards) in a waterproof bag close to you
  • Some day centres and hostels have lockers, ask about this
  • Photograph important documents and email them to yourself so you have a digital copy if anything is lost

Getting an ID without a home address

Not having ID can feel like a barrier to everything. But there are legal ways to get documents without a fixed address then this guide explains how.

Why you need ID

Without identification, it can be difficult to:

  • Open a bank account
  • Access benefits
  • Register with a GP
  • Access emergency accommodation
  • Get a job

Birth certificate

If you don't have your birth certificate, you can order a replacement from the General Register Office (GRO).

Cost: £11 online

Website: www.gov.uk/order-copy-birth-death-marriage-certificate

You don't need a fixed address to apply and you can use a day centre, a friend's address, or ask Porchlight staff to receive the letter on your behalf.

Passport

You can apply for or renew a passport without a permanent address. Use a trusted address such as a day centre, hostel, or friend's address as your contact address.

Cost: From £88.50 for an adult passport (as of 2024)

Website: www.gov.uk/apply-renew-passport

If cost is a barrier, speak to Porchlight there are funds available to help people in need get ID documents.

Proof of address when you have no address

Some things (like opening a bank account) ask for proof of address. If you have no address, there are alternatives:

  • A letter from Porchlight or another support organisation confirming your situation is accepted by some banks and services
  • Basic bank accounts (such as those from Monzo, Starling, or high street banks offering "basic" accounts) often have relaxed ID requirements, ask specifically about their policy for people with no fixed abode
  • The government's One Login scheme (www.gov.uk/using-your-gov-uk-one-login) can confirm your identity digitally for some services

National Insurance number

If you've lost your National Insurance (NI) number, you can find it on old payslips, P60s, or letters from HMRC. If you can't find it, call HMRC on 0300 200 3500.

You don't need a permanent address to contact HMRC, they can send correspondence to a day centre or support worker's address.

Getting help with this process

Navigating ID applications can feel overwhelming, especially when you're dealing with so much else. Porchlight staff and support workers can:

  • Help you understand what ID you need
  • Write supporting letters
  • Receive documents on your behalf
  • Point you toward emergency funds for ID costs

Call 0800 567 7699 or visit our interactive map to find a drop-in near you.

Claiming benefits

Benefits can feel like a maze, but many people experiencing homelessness are entitled to them and just need help accessing them. You don't need to have a permanent address to claim most benefits.

Universal Credit

Universal Credit is the main benefit for working-age people who are on a low income or out of work. It covers:

  • Living costs
  • Housing costs (if you're in temporary accommodation)
  • Support if you have a health condition or disability

To claim: www.gov.uk/universal-credit/how-to-claim you'll need a bank account, an email address, and some form of ID. If any of these are a problem, see our guide on getting ID without a home address, or speak to a Porchlight support worker who can help you apply.

If you have no address: You can use a day centre or support service address when making your claim. Let the DWP (Department for Work and Pensions) know you are homeless, this can affect how your claim is processed and what housing support you receive.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

If you have a long-term health condition, disability, or mental health difficulty, you may be entitled to PIP. It's not means-tested, you can claim it whether or not you work or receive other benefits.

To claim: Call 0800 917 2222 or visit www.gov.uk/pip

Housing Benefit / Housing element of Universal Credit

If you're placed in temporary accommodation or a hostel by the council, your housing costs may be covered through Universal Credit's housing element or Housing Benefit. Your support worker or the hostel staff can usually help with this.

Discretionary Housing Payments

If your benefits don't fully cover your rent, you can ask your local council for a Discretionary Housing Payment (DHP), a top-up to help with housing costs.

Getting help to claim

Benefits forms and processes can be complicated. Free help is available from:

  • Citizens Advice Kent - find your local office at www.citizensadvice.org.uk
  • Porchlight support workers - call 0800 567 7699
  • Shelter's benefits advice line - 0808 800 4444

You don't have to do this alone. A support worker can sit with you to complete forms, make phone calls on your behalf, and help you appeal if a claim is refused.

If a claim is refused

Don't give up. Many decisions are overturned on appeal. You have the right to:

  1. Ask for a mandatory reconsideration (within one month of the decision)
  2. Appeal to an independent tribunal if the reconsideration is unsuccessful

Citizens Advice can support you through this process for free.

Recognising a crisis

A mental health crisis is any situation where your emotional or psychological distress becomes so intense that you feel unable to cope. Crises are not a sign of weakness or failure. They happen to people and there is help available.

What a crisis might feel like

Emotionally

  • Feeling completely overwhelmed, numb, or like you've "shut down"
  • Intense fear, panic, or dread that won't go away
  • Feeling like you're losing your grip on reality
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or not wanting to be alive

Physically

  • Inability to eat, sleep, or carry out basic daily tasks
  • Heart racing, shaking, difficulty breathing
  • Feeling frozen or unable to move

In your thoughts

  • Thoughts racing uncontrollably, or going very slow and foggy
  • Believing things that feel very real but may not be (e.g. that you are in danger, that people are against you)
  • Thoughts that feel repetitive and impossible to stop

In your behaviour

  • Withdrawing completely from other people
  • Acting out of character in ways that feel unlike you
  • Using substances to cope in ways that feel out of control

What is NOT a crisis (but still matters)

You don't have to be in crisis for your mental health to matter or for you to deserve support. Feeling persistently low, anxious, angry, or empty (even without a single dramatic moment) is a signal worth taking seriously.

If you're not in crisis but you're not okay, please still reach out. See our guide on creating your own personal crisis plan to help you prepare before things get harder.

Getting help in a crisis in Kent

  • Kent and Medway Mental Health Crisis Line: 0800 107 0160 - free, 24 hours a day, 7 days a week
  • Samaritans: 116 123 - free, 24/7, confidential, for when you need to talk to someone
  • Text KENT to 85258 - free 24/7 text crisis support
  • 111, option 2 - mental health crisis support via NHS 111
  • 999 or A&E - if you are in immediate danger

How to calm yourself during panic or overwhelm

When anxiety or panic takes hold, your body goes into emergency mode. This guide explains what's happening and gives you tools to help bring yourself back.

What's happening in your body

When you feel panicked or overwhelmed, your brain triggers the "fight or flight" response which releases adrenaline, speeding up your heart and making you breathe faster. This response evolved to help us escape danger. In a panic attack, it fires when there's no physical threat and the physical sensations (racing heart, dizziness, trembling) can themselves become frightening, making the panic worse.

Understanding this can help. Your body is not broken. It is doing what it is designed to do and you can help it return to calm.

Controlled breathing

Panic causes fast, shallow breathing, which makes symptoms worse. Slowing your breath signals safety to your nervous system.

Box breathing

  1. Breathe in for 4 counts
  2. Hold for 4 counts
  3. Breathe out for 4 counts
  4. Hold for 4 counts

Repeat 4 times.

4-7-8 breathing

  1. Breathe in for 4 counts
  2. Hold for 7 counts
  3. Breathe out slowly for 8 counts

The long exhale is the most important part as it activates the body's calming system.

Physical grounding

  • Press your feet into the floor. Really feel the weight of your body.
  • Hold something cold like an ice cube, a cold glass of water. The sensation can interrupt the panic spiral.
  • Splash cold water on your face. This activates the "dive reflex" and can quickly lower heart rate.
  • Move your body. Even walking slowly around the room helps discharge adrenaline.

Remind yourself: this will pass

Panic peaks within about 10 minutes and then starts to subside even if it doesn't feel that way in the moment. You are not in danger. You have survived this before. It will not last forever.

If panic attacks are frequent

Panic attacks that happen regularly are treatable. Talk to your GP about what you're experiencing. Therapy (particularly Cognitive Behavioural Therapy) is effective and available on the NHS. You can also refer yourself directly in Kent through Increasing Access to Psychological Therapies (IAPT) just search "Kent IAPT self referral" or ask your GP.

Dealing with suicidal thoughts

If you're having thoughts of suicide or of not wanting to be alive, please know: you are not alone, and these thoughts do not have to lead to action. Help is available right now.

If you are in immediate danger

Please reach out right now:

  • 999 - if you are in immediate danger
  • Kent and Medway Mental Health Crisis Line: 0800 107 0160 - 24 hours, free
  • Samaritans: 116 123 - 24/7, free, confidential
  • Text KENT to 85258 - free, 24/7 text support
  • A&E / your nearest emergency department

You don't have to be "sure enough" to call. If you're having these thoughts, that is enough reason to reach out.

Understanding suicidal thoughts

Suicidal thoughts are more common than most people realise, and having them does not mean you are "crazy" or that you will act on them. They are often a signal that pain has become unbearable and that something needs to change.

Having a thought is not the same as taking action. Many people experience suicidal thoughts and go on to recover and live full lives.

What to do when the thoughts arrive

1. Don't be alone if you can help it.
Contact a friend, a family member, or a helpline. Being with another person or voice can provide enough distance from the thoughts to get through the moment.
2. Create distance from means.
If you have thought about how you might hurt yourself, try to put physical distance between yourself and anything you were thinking about using. Ask someone to hold onto medication. Leave the place you're in.
3. Use a grounding technique.
See our guide on how to calm yourself during panic or overwhelm, grounding can help interrupt the intensity of suicidal thoughts in the moment.
4. Ride the wave.
Suicidal crises tend to peak and then subside. If you can get through the next hour, the intensity often lifts. Call a helpline, watch something, go somewhere, anything to put time between the thought and any action.
5. Make it harder to act.
If you have a plan, tell someone. Remove access to the means you were thinking about. This single step saves lives.

Talk to someone you trust

You don't have to explain everything perfectly. You can simply say: "I'm not doing okay. I'm having thoughts of hurting myself." That's enough. People who care about you would want to know.

Getting longer-term support

Suicidal thoughts are a signal that you need more support. Please speak to your GP as a first step, they can refer you to mental health services. In Kent, you can also self-refer to talking therapies (search "Kent IAPT self referral").

If you are homeless or in housing crisis, please know that your mental health still matters and you deserve care. Porchlight can help connect you to support - call 0800 567 7699.

Supporting someone else

Watching someone you care about struggle is painful and confusing. This guide is for anyone supporting a friend, family member, or someone they've just met who is in crisis or struggling with their mental health.

If someone is in immediate danger

If you believe someone is about to harm themselves:

  • Stay with them if it is safe to do so
  • Call 999
  • Remove access to anything they might use to harm themselves if you can do so safely
  • Call the Kent and Medway Crisis Line on 0800 107 0160 for advice

The most important thing you can do

Listen, just to be present and hear them.

Many people in crisis don't need solutions. They need to feel less alone. Your presence, your willingness to stay, is more powerful than you might think.

How to start the conversation

It can feel frightening to bring up mental health or suicidal thoughts, many people worry they'll "make it worse." Research consistently shows this is not true. Asking someone directly if they are thinking about suicide does not plant the idea; it often brings relief.

You could say:

  • "I've noticed you haven't seemed yourself lately. Are you okay?"
  • "I'm worried about you. Can you tell me how you're really doing?"
  • "I'm not going to judge you, I just want to understand what you're going through."

How to listen well

  • Give them time. Don't rush to fill silences.
  • Don't minimise. Avoid phrases like "it could be worse" or "you have so much to be grateful for" as however well-intentioned, these tend to make people feel more alone.
  • Reflect back. "It sounds like things have been really hard" is more helpful than jumping straight to advice.
  • Ask what they need. "What would be most helpful for you right now?" sometimes the answer is just "someone to sit with me."
  • Believe them. If someone says they're struggling, take it seriously.

Encouraging them to get help

You can support someone toward professional help without forcing them. You might offer to:

  • Help them find a GP or call for an appointment
  • Go with them to an appointment
  • Look up local services together
  • Sit with them while they call a helpline

If they're not ready, keep the door open. Sometimes knowing one person cares is enough to keep someone going until they're ready.

Looking after yourself

Supporting someone in crisis is hard. You may feel scared, helpless, drained, or even angry. All of these feelings are understandable, please, don't neglect your own wellbeing.

  • You are not responsible for another person's choices
  • You cannot force someone to get help, and you are not failing if they don't
  • Talk to someone yourself: a friend, a GP, or the Samaritans (116 123)
  • Carers in Kent can access support through Carers First: 0300 303 1555

Creating your own personal crisis plan

A crisis plan is something you create when you're calm, so that it's ready when things get hard. It's one of the most effective things you can do to protect yourself.

What is a crisis plan?

A crisis plan (sometimes called a "safety plan") is a written or digital document, personal to you, that helps you know what to do when things feel unmanageable. It works because it removes the need to think clearly in a moment when thinking clearly is hardest.

You don't have to be in crisis to make one. In fact, it's best to make one when you're having a reasonably okay day.

What to include in your crisis plan

You can use this as a template. Add to it, change it, make it yours.

1. My warning signs

What are the early signals that things are starting to get harder for me?

Examples: I stop replying to messages. I can't sleep. I feel completely numb. I start drinking more.

2. Things that help me feel better

What has worked in the past, even a little?

Examples: Walking to the park. A specific playlist. Calling [name]. Having a shower. Watching [TV show].

3. Things that make things worse

What should I avoid when I'm struggling?

Examples: Scrolling social media. Drinking. Being in certain places or around certain people.

4. People I can contact

Who in my life can I reach out to when things feel bad?

5. Professional support I have

Examples: my GP name and number, my support worker, my therapist

6. Crisis lines I can call

  • Samaritans: 116 123 (24/7, free)
  • Kent and Medway Mental Health Crisis Line: 0800 107 0160 (24/7, free)
  • Text KENT to 85258 (24/7, free)
  • Porchlight: 0800 567 7699 (Monday to Friday, 9am to 5pm, free.)

7. What I want people to know if I'm in crisis

This can be a note to a friend, a family member, or emergency services. Anything that would help them understand and support you.

8. Reasons to stay safe

What matters to you? What do you have to look forward to, even something small?

Review it regularly

Your crisis plan is a living document. As your life and circumstances change, update it. Some people revisit theirs every few months.

Getting help to make one

A support worker, therapist, or GP can help you build your crisis plan. Porchlight staff can also work through this with you - call 0800 567 7699.
